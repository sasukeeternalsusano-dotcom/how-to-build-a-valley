The Making of Danoya Valley

how a little world for two was built, one tiny square at a time

Danoya Valley is a five chapter game I made for Danoya. It runs in any web browser, on a phone or a laptop, and every picture, sound and story beat in it was planned, drawn, written and tested by me. I did the work, and it took over a month from the first idea to the finished game. I used YouTube and Claude AI to assist me along the way.

Part 2 · My toolbox

What I built it with

No game engine, no paid software. The whole game is plain web code, so the finished thing is just a folder of files that any browser can open. These are the tools I used, in plain words.

The working folder grew to about 40 code files. Before every change I saved a backup copy of the file I was about to touch (like ch4.bak_v181.js), so with close to 190 versions, any mistake could be undone in seconds.

Part 3 · How the pieces fit

One engine, five chapters stacked on top

Think of the game like a layer cake. At the bottom is one shared "engine" that every chapter uses: the world, the walking, the talking, the saving. Each chapter is a new layer added on top, which can change how things work without touching the layers underneath. That's how Chapter 1 stayed exactly as it was while the later chapters grew. Tap a layer.

The stapler. A small script I called buildpub.sh takes the right pieces for each chapter, in the right order, and staples them into one page: chapter1.html to chapter5.html. After every staple, a checker read each page for typos in the code, so a mistake never reached the real game.

The memory boxes

The game remembers things in the browser's own little storage. Some memories travel from one chapter to the next, which is how a choice she makes early can show up at the very end.

The cutscene selector. Every scene has its own little address, like chapter3.html#cut-funeral. Opening that jumps straight to the scene in a "preview" mode: nothing is saved, minigames finish themselves, and a Back button takes you back to the list on the right chapter. It made testing and rewriting scenes far faster.

How I'd build it again, step by step

  
    
    
  1. Write the story backwards. Decide the ending, then the three or four things the ending needs the player to already know, then where each one gets hidden. One page of plan per chapter.
    2. 
    
  2. Make one screen work: a map, a character that walks, and a talking box. That's the skeleton above.
    3. 
    
  3. Add one thing at a time and play after each: pressing A on things, then items and a bag, then saving, then a clock, then villagers. Each should work before the next one starts.
    4. 
    
  4. Write scenes as lists of lines, so the story is easy to read, pull out and rewrite.
    5. 
    
  5. Split the code into files once one file gets long, and use a build script to staple them into one page per chapter.
    6. 
    
  6. Save with a version number in the name, and always "try" before reading a save, so a broken save never crashes the game.
    7. 
    
  7. Add a cutscene selector early. Jumping straight to any scene makes testing much faster.
    8. 
    
  8. Test with scripts and with your hands, on the phone the player will actually use.

  Part 5 · How the pictures work
  
Everything is made of coloured squares

  
There isn't a single picture file in the whole game. A computer screen is just tiny squares of colour, so I described every character, tree and house as a list of coloured rectangles: "paint a brown box here, a blue box there". The game paints that list sixty times a second.

  

    
    

      
0 rectangles

      
This is Danoya exactly as the game draws her, one rectangle at a time. Her scarf, face, jacket, shoes and even the shadow under her feet are each just a few boxes.

      

    

  

  
R(px+10, py+2, 12, 9, "#a8683f");   // her face: 12 wide, 9 tall, warm brown

  
That one line is a real line from the game. The numbers say where the box goes and how big it is; the last part is the colour.

  

    
Trees, houses, fences, the stove, the shrine are each a short list of these boxes. Because nothing is a picture file, the whole game stays tiny and any change shows up instantly.

    
Light is soft circles. Lamps, lanterns, campfires and the spirit glow are see through circles that fade at the edges. Night is a dark blue layer laid over the whole map, with the lamps punched through it.

  

  
Every chapter has its own colours

  
Ch 1 & 2pink and coral, warm and cosy
Ch 3teal, for the caves
Ch 4violet, for the spirit world
Ch 5amber, for the ending

  
Small effects carry a lot of the feeling: drifting sakura petals, fireflies at night, the gentle bob of a lantern, and the Name Moth badge slowly shimmering on her coat.

  Part 7 · The story
  
I wrote it backwards

  
I decided the ending first, then worked out what the player needed to already know when they got there, then hid those clues in the cosy early chapters. Almost nothing in Chapter 1 is filler.

  
The world in a few lines. Long ago, the valley's Keepers kept a road of lanterns from the south cliffs to a Door under the north hill, every lantern lit from one crystal, the Sunheart. The last Keeper could not sleep. His wife, a lantern maker who signed her work with a single letter, S, lit the first lantern ever made for a living person, so that he might rest. When she died, he refused to open the Door, and it has been knocking, politely, ever since. Sixty years ago a Great Storm brought the south cliffs down across the harbour, buried the Southern Gate, stopped the ferry, and took one boy. Since then the valley buys its festival lanterns instead of making them, a fox's name has been forgotten, and the lamps are slowly dimming.

  
Clues and where they pay off

  
Tap a card to see where each clue comes back.

  

  

    
    

    
The lullaby Danoya hums without knowing where she learned it. The same three falling notes play when the bees settle, on her lantern, and when the Door finally answers.

  

  
My rules for the writing

  

    
Every clue gets a payoff. If someone mentions something twice, it has to matter later.

    
People only know what they could know. Doodi is new to the valley, so he says "Ferris says…" instead of knowing it himself.

    
Small moments beat big ones. A cup poured for an empty chair, a dog finally bringing back a stick, three knocks on a door.

    
Names are held back. The library never names the last Keeper. His name is scratched out of every page, and you only learn it in Chapter 5.

    
Jokes stay light around grief. Chapter 3 opens on a funeral, so I cut a dozen dungeon jokes that undercut it.

  

  
How I wrote it and checked it

  
    
    
  1. A one page plan per chapter first: what happens, what the player learns, and which clues get hidden.
    2. 
    
  2. Lines live inside the scenes. Every line is written as "who says it" plus "what they say", right where it happens, so I could see each line in its moment.
    3. 
    
  3. Read it like a book. I pulled every line of dialogue into one document per chapter and read it top to bottom at least five times, each time hunting one kind of mistake: the order of events, who knows what, repeated jokes, the tone, and clues that never paid off.
    4. 
    
  4. Check every fact against every chapter: dates, ages, who was where during the Storm, what the shrine looks like.
    5. 
    
  5. Play it. Some lines only sound wrong when they pop up in the middle of a scene.
    6. 
  







  Part 8 · Music, sound and the personal touches
  
49 songs, chosen scene by scene

  
Every area, date, fight and cutscene has its own song picked for it, so the music changes with the mood instead of looping one tune. I checked the music the same way I checked the words: my test scripts wrote down which song played in every cutscene, and I fixed every mismatch, like the ending credits starting on the calm town theme.

  

    The little sounds are made by code, not recorded.
    
Every bleep, chime and footstep is a tiny tone the game makes on the spot. Tap one to hear it.

    

    
Phones only allow sound after you tap something, so in the game the music always starts from a button press.

  Part 10 · Testing
  
A robot player, then me

  
I tested two ways. The robot catches what breaks; my own playing catches what feels wrong.

  
    
    
  1. The robot watched all 52 cutscenes. A tool called Playwright opens a real browser and clicks for you. My scripts opened every scene, clicked through every line, took screenshots along the way, wrote down which song played, and checked the Back button went to the right place. The screenshots were stitched into one big sheet per chapter so I could scan a whole chapter in seconds.
    2. 
    
  2. The robot played the minigames. It tapped exactly at the edge of the green, in the middle, and just outside, to prove the timing was fair.
    3. 
    
  3. The robot tested single things. For example: stand next to a bench, face it, press A, and check the sitting text appears.
    4. 
    
  4. The robot pretended to be a phone, with touch, a narrow screen and slow internet, to catch problems that only happen on a phone.
    5. 
    
  5. Then I played it all on my phone and wrote down anything off in plain words, like "the jar sometimes doesn't count when I'm on the green" or "Doodi and Judy are sitting on a beehive". Every note became a fix in the next version. The joystick and the side quests came straight from that.
    6.

  Part 11 · Putting it online
  
A real website, for free

  
The finished game lives on GitHub Pages: 8 page files plus a folder of 49 songs, about 93 MB in total. Anyone with the link can play it on a phone or laptop.

  
    
    
  1. Pack it. Copy the finished files into one clean folder. Every page needs two small lines at the top, or emoji turn into gibberish.
    2. 
    
  2. Make a repository on github.com (an online folder) and set it to Public, which free accounts need for this.
    3. 
    
  3. Upload the pages with "Add file" then "Upload files", so the menu page sits at the very top.
    4. 
    
  4. Make the music folder with "Create new file" and type audio/readme.txt. The slash is what makes a folder.
    5. 
    
  5. Upload the songs 5 to 10 at a time. GitHub quietly drops big batches. One batch of mine failed, ten songs went missing, and some scenes played in silence.
    6. 
    
  6. Check every song name against the list. Names must match exactly, even capital letters.
    7. 
    
  7. Turn on Pages in Settings, wait a minute or two for the green tick, and the folder becomes a website with its own link.
    8. 
  

  
💾 Saves live in the browser on that device, so a phone and a laptop each keep their own progress.
🕶️ Private browsing forgets the save when it closes.
🔕 A phone's silent switch mutes the music.
